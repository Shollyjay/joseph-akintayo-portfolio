107 automations, 48 of them switched off, and no lifecycle holding them together

A full GoHighLevel audit of an account with the automation count of a mature system and the structure of a new one: 107 workflows, 45% inactive, many with no trigger at all, and no customer lifecycle connecting any of it.

What the account had, against what it was missing

The situation

The account did not look neglected. It looked busy. 107 automations, integrations into Zapier, n8n and a set of webhooks, a lead scoring system, AI tools, a pipeline, a WordPress site feeding leads in. By every surface measure it was a mature GoHighLevel build.

It behaved like an early-stage one. Leads arrived and were not consistently followed up. Staff were unclear who owned what. The owner could not answer basic questions about where leads came from or where they stalled.

The brief was to find out why, and to say so in writing.

What the audit found

The headline number is the one that reframes the whole engagement: of 107 automations, 59 were published and 48 were drafts. Forty-five percent of the system was switched off, and nobody knew which half was which.

Workflows with no trigger

Several automations — both draft and live — had no entry condition at all. They could never fire. They sat in the list looking like capability.

Automations running in isolation

The live workflows were largely standalone. None passed a contact to the next step, so there was no path through the system, only a set of disconnected events.

WordPress leads arriving nowhere

Leads came in from the website with no consistent workflow entry point, no standard tagging and no immediate response. They were captured, then effectively parked.

A manual pipeline

Stages existed but were moved by hand. Nothing automated stage movement, so the pipeline recorded work someone remembered to do rather than driving it.

Lead scoring with no consequence

Scores were being calculated and then ignored. They changed no routing, no priority and no follow-up intensity, so a high-intent lead was treated exactly like a cold one.

No task or ownership layer

No automatic task creation, no staff assignment, no completion tracking. Accountability depended entirely on memory.

Logic scattered outside the CRM

Zapier, n8n and raw webhooks were doing work GoHighLevel could do natively, which multiplied the number of places a failure could hide.

AI bolted on rather than built in

AI tools were present but not embedded in any workflow, firing at the wrong points in the journey rather than at first response or qualification.

The actual problem

Ten problems were listed, but they were symptoms of one. The system had been built as a collection of tools and automations rather than as a connected workflow system reflecting how the business actually converts a lead into a patient.

That distinction decides what you do next. If the problem is missing features, you buy and build more. If the problem is missing structure, more building makes it worse — and this account was the proof, because it had already built 107 times.

What I recommended

A lifecycle-based workflow system: not one central workflow, but several connected ones, each owning a stage and handing off to the next.

The lifecycle

Lead → Engage → Book → Show → Convert → Nurture → Reactivate, with a dedicated workflow per stage rather than one monolith trying to branch its way through everything.

A contract for every workflow

Each one trigger-based, updating the pipeline stage, applying and removing its tags, creating tasks where a human is needed, and explicitly passing the contact to the next workflow. That last clause is what turns a set of automations into a system.

The pipeline demoted to a view

Stage movement automated by the workflows, so the pipeline becomes a tracking surface rather than a data entry chore.

Scoring wired to consequences

Scores driving priority handling, staff notification and follow-up intensity, so the number finally changes what happens to the lead.

Logic pulled back into GoHighLevel

Reducing webhook and external-tool dependency where the platform can do the work, cutting the number of failure points.

The roadmap

Seven phases, deliberately ordered so that nothing gets built on top of a mess: system mapping first, then tag cleanup, then the workflow build, then pipeline and task automation, then integration consolidation, then AI, and reporting last.

Mapping is phase one for the same reason the audit existed. The account had never had its ideal process written down, so every previous build had been a guess about what the system should do.

Where it landed

The client received a ten-page audit and a one-page summary they could act on, with the automation inventory quantified, ten named problems, a target architecture and a phased plan.

The conclusion was the part worth paying for: the system had the volume of a mature setup but not the structure of one, and the next phase was not more tools. It was organising what already existed into something connected.
